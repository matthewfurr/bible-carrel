What changes: the name set like the book's title, the facts in the same aligned list as About, and a choice above the list: Most named or A to Z (Places gets the same control). Under the name, the data's own label where it has one ("son of Boaz", "father of Abijah"); David has none. The data records fathers and mothers but nobody's sex, so the app never writes "son" or "daughter" itself. Cost: small, mostly reuse of About's pieces.

The family tree

What the data can and cannot say: who is a parent, partner, sibling, half-sibling or child. It does not record anyone's sex or birth order, so the tree cannot say "brother" or "eldest". It records the mother of 10 of David's 21 children. Grandparents are reached by walking up through a parent.

A classic tree, on both

Family · scrolls sideways ⟷
What it is: what a family tree looks like on paper: generations top to bottom, joined by lines. The person's siblings hang from the parent, partners from the person, and children from their mother where the data names her. Children whose mother is not named sit together underneath. Every name is a link that walks the tree.
Cost: the largest. It needs a layout of our own that draws the connecting lines, scrolls sideways when a family is wider than the column (on the phone that is most families), and gives VoiceOver its own reading order. David's tree is about the widest the data has; Jacob's (4 partners, 13 children) fits.

What it is: the line of descent read downwards, as the Bible's genealogies read (grandfather, father, the person), with the siblings at the father's level, then the household: each partner with her children under her. It uses the timeline's rail, so the two read as one system.

What it is: A's tree, kept inside the column: when a generation is too wide, the overflow folds into a count you open in place (siblings, the last partners, the children whose mother is not named). The phone gets B, since a drawn tree at phone width folds almost everything.
